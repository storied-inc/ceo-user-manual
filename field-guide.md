# CEO User Manual
## The field guide

A CEO User Manual is a structured document that makes explicit how you think, decide, and communicate, so the people who work with you and the AI acting on your behalf can operate with you instead of around you.

This guide is the method behind it: what the two layers are, what separates a section someone can act on from a section that sounds fine and changes nothing, the four ways these documents fail, and how to hand one to a team so it actually gets used.

It's worth reading on its own, independent of whether you ever write the document.

---

## What the document is for

Right now your operating system exists in fragments. A little of it in each colleague's head, assembled by watching you, none of it written, and none of it in any AI you use. Every new person rebuilds their copy from scratch, and every one of them builds a slightly different one.

That distributed guesswork has a price, and you pay it in translation. Work comes back near-miss rather than wrong. Decisions route around you or wait for you. The same clarification gets requested by four people who never compare notes. None of it is anyone's fault, and all of it is fixable by writing one document.

The document consolidates it. That's the whole mechanism, and it's why the return is disproportionate to the effort. You spend thirty minutes once. Everyone who works with you stops spending weeks.

## The two layers

Every manual worth installing runs two layers at once, and they do different jobs.

**The universal layer.** How you think and decide. First principles, decision heuristics, how you frame a problem before solving it, how you separate signal from noise, what you mean by something working. This layer upgrades anyone who reads it. Someone finishes it and they're a better operator, whether or not they ever work with you, because you've handed them a way of thinking rather than a set of preferences.

**The personal layer.** You. Your voice, your quirks, your specific triggers, how you actually want information delivered, the five to seven things you learned the hard way and don't want anyone to learn that way again.

Skip the second and you've written a leadership template with your name on it. Nobody recognizes you in it, so nobody changes anything after reading it. Skip the first and you've written a list of preferences, which trains people to manage your moods rather than to think.

Aim for at least a third of the finished document to be unmistakably you. The test is a sentence: someone reads it and thinks, that's so you, I knew that intuitively and nobody had ever written it down.

## What separates a real section from a section that sounds fine

This is the craft of the whole document, and it comes down to one rule. **Every principle carries an observable behavior.**

Weak, and extremely common:

> I value clear thinking and structured communication. I appreciate when people come prepared.

Nobody can act on that. It's true, it's agreeable, and it changes nothing, because every reader already believes they communicate clearly and come prepared. The sentence gives them no way to find out they're wrong.

Strong, from Michael's own manual:

> If you come to me with "I'm not sure what to do," I will respond with "What do you recommend and why?" You do not need perfect answers. You need structured thinking.

Same principle. Now it's a prediction about what will happen in a specific moment, which means a reader can check themselves against it before the meeting rather than during it.

The difference is that the second version names a behavior on both sides. What you'll do, and what they should do instead. Every section in [`template.md`](template.md) should be readable as a prediction someone could test.

### The evidence question

One question runs underneath all eight sections. **When did this last happen?**

You don't have to publish the answer. You do have to have one. A principle with no instance behind it is a preference wearing better clothes, and the reason this matters more than it used to is in the first failure mode below.

## The four failure modes

### 1. A leader who doesn't exist

The document describes who you'd like to be rather than how you operate. This is the most common failure and now the most expensive one.

Your team absorbs an aspirational manual and corrects for it silently. Someone reads that you want the recommendation rather than the problem, watches you interrupt three recommendations to ask for the underlying data, and updates their private model without mentioning it. The document loses authority and nobody tells you.

An AI can't do that. It has no private model to update. It takes what you wrote as fact and operates as that person in every output, indefinitely, with total fidelity to a description of someone who isn't there.

**The fix.** Run the aspiration sweep before you install. Read the document once looking only for sentences you'd like to be true. Cut them or make them true.

### 2. The values list

Abstraction with nothing attached. "I value transparency." "I believe in ownership." "I care about quality."

These aren't wrong, they're inert. They describe a disposition rather than a behavior, so the reader nods and does exactly what they were going to do anyway.

**The fix.** For each one, answer two questions in the document. What does this look like when someone does it well? What does it look like when it gets violated? If you can't answer the second, you don't have a principle yet.

### 3. The borrowed manual

Someone else's document with your name substituted in. It happens because a good example is genuinely useful and the temptation to keep the language is strong.

Michael handed his manual to three peers in an advisory group. Inside 48 hours, all three had sent it to their own teams with most of his language intact. The document worked, and it wasn't theirs. The personal layer belonged to someone their readers had never met.

**The fix.** Use an example for structure and depth. Write the sentences yourself. The exercise is the articulation, which is also where all the value is.

### 4. The finished manual

Written once, dated never, revised never. Reality moves, the document doesn't, and eventually it's a confident description of a company that no longer exists. Worse than silence, because people act on it.

**The fix.** Version it and date it the way you would any document your team relies on. Michael's is on V2.2 and it changed because people told him where it was wrong. Reread it when something breaks twice.

## Keep the contradictions

When two answers in your manual disagree, resist the urge to smooth them into something consistent.

Michael's manual says he moves fast once he has clarity, and it also says he thinks out loud constantly and that thinking out loud isn't instruction. Those sit in tension. A reader has to work out whether a given statement is a directive or an exploration, which is a real problem they face every week.

Naming the tension is what makes it manageable. His document handles it by giving the reader a move: before executing on something he said, confirm whether it's a directive or an idea.

The places where a leader's stated principles collide are exactly the places their team burns the most energy guessing. Written down, with a move attached, they become the most valuable lines on the page. Smoothed over, they stay expensive.

## Handing it to your team

The document does nothing in a folder. Two destinations, and the sequence matters.

**One person first.** Give it to someone who works closely with you and ask a single question: does this match your experience of me? Then stay quiet while they answer. Don't explain, don't contextualize, don't defend the sentence they're questioning. The gaps between what you wrote and what they've experienced are the fastest, highest-value edit you will ever get on this document, and they evaporate the moment you start justifying.

**Then everyone.** Send it with one line about what it's for and an explicit standing invitation to tell you where it's wrong. Put it wherever your team already looks rather than in an attachment, because a document people have to find is a document people don't read.

**Then your leaders write theirs.** This is the part with the compounding return. One manual makes you legible. A manual from each leader, for the team that reports to them, makes the organization legible, and that's a different order of value. It also solves the problem your manual can't reach: day-to-day friction usually isn't with you, it's with someone's direct manager.

For the AI half of the install, see [`install-in-your-ai.md`](install-in-your-ai.md).

## Where this doesn't work

Worth knowing the limits.

It doesn't fix a trust problem. If people are working around you because they don't trust your judgment or your reactions, a document explaining your judgment will read as a demand for compliance. Fix the relationship first.

It doesn't substitute for management. A manual tells someone how you operate. It doesn't tell them whether their work is good, and it can become a way to avoid saying so.

And it doesn't survive contact with a leader who won't be corrected. The recognition test only works if the answer is allowed to be no. If nobody on your team believes they can tell you the document is wrong, you'll get agreement instead of information, and the manual will slowly become fiction that everyone has agreed to endorse.
