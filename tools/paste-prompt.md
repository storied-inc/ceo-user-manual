# The Prompt

No setup. Paste everything between the rules into Claude or ChatGPT, in a tool that already knows something about how you work. You'll get a V1 back, plus the questions that make V2 sharper.

If you're starting cold, in a tool with no history of you, use the interview version at the bottom instead.

---

I'm building a CEO User Manual: a structured document that makes explicit how I think, decide, and communicate, so the people who work with me and the AI acting on my behalf can operate with me rather than around me. It installs in two places, my team and my AI, and it has to work in both.

Draft V1 from everything you know about me: how I think, how I work, how I decide, how I define and measure success. Cover principles and preferences both, and say which is which, because most teams can't tell the difference and treat both as immovable.

**The spine. Eight sections.** How I make decisions. How I communicate. How to succeed working with me. What triggers me, meaning friction signals rather than pet peeves. What energizes me. What I need from the people around me. How I give and receive feedback. My honest, unfiltered truths, including quirks, contradictions, and what people misunderstand about me. Drop a section if you have nothing real for it and tell me which one you dropped. An evenly covered manual is usually one nobody had to think hard about.

**Two layers, and I need both.** The universal layer is how I think and decide, and it should upgrade anyone who reads it. The personal layer is me: my voice, my quirks, the things people learn about me the hard way. At least 30% of the finished document has to be unmistakably me, not a leadership template with my name on it. Any reader should instantly recognize me, and should know what to do differently after reading.

**Define everything behaviorally.** Every bullet and every concept gets a clear why, or a description of what it looks like in behavior. A principle with no observable behavior attached is lost in abstraction and nobody can act on it. "I value clear thinking" is useless. "If you bring me a problem without a recommendation, I'll ask what you recommend and why, and we'll spend the meeting on that instead" is usable.

**Attach evidence.** For each claim, ask yourself whether you can point to an instance. Where you're drafting from something I've actually said or done, keep it specific. Where you're guessing or pattern-matching from leaders in general, mark the line clearly so I can confirm or kill it. Do not smooth a guess into a confident sentence. The most dangerous thing in this document is a description of who I want to be instead of how I actually operate, because my team will quietly correct for that and my AI never will.

**Use my language.** Not polished corporate rewrites. If I say something bluntly, keep it blunt. If I have a phrase I actually use, use it.

**Flag the contradictions.** Where my stated principles collide, name the collision instead of resolving it into something smoother. Those are the places my team spends the most energy guessing, which makes them the most valuable lines on the page.

**Two rules that bind your output, not just mine.** Don't open by telling me this is a great exercise or a smart question. No em dashes.

**Then stop and ask me questions.** After V1, give me no more than five questions that would make V2 significantly sharper. Push on anything that reads as generic, anything that could apply to any leader, and anything that sounds aspirational rather than actual. Ask what a principle looks like when it gets violated. Ask when something last happened.

**End the document with three to five validation questions** I should put to someone who works closely with me, to test whether this matches their experience of me.

The bar: could someone read this in ten minutes and understand how I think and work, without a single meeting?

## If your AI doesn't know you yet

Same document, built by interview instead. Replace the opening instruction above with this one, and keep everything else.

> Don't draft anything yet. Interview me first, one question at a time, working through the eight sections above. After each answer, ask one follow-up that pushes deeper. If an answer sounds like it could apply to any leader, tell me it's too generic and ask again. If I give you a corporate answer like "I value transparency," ask what that actually looks like in practice, when it's gotten me in trouble, and what the version of it is that only my closest colleagues would recognize. Keep going until my answers are specific enough that someone who works with me would read them and say "that's exactly right." Then compile.

Expect it to take longer and produce a better document. Run the draft version first if you can, and use the interview to fix what it got wrong.
