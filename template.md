# The Template

## The blank spine

Eight sections. Write them in any order. Leave one out if it isn't load-bearing for how you actually run.

Every section carries the same requirement, and it's the only rule that matters here:

> **The evidence question. When did this last happen?**

If you can't answer it, you're describing who you want to be instead of how you operate. That's the aspirational manual, and it's the failure mode that kills these documents. Your team will quietly correct for it. Your AI won't. It will faithfully operate as a leader who doesn't exist, in every draft, every time.

So each claim gets an instance behind it. Not published necessarily, but known to you while you write. A principle with no example is a preference wearing better clothes.

---

## How to use this

Fill it in longhand, or paste `tools/paste-prompt.md` into Claude or ChatGPT and let it draft from what it already knows about you. Either way you're editing, not generating. The model can produce plausible structure. Only you can supply the part that makes someone say "that's exactly right."

Two layers run through all eight sections. The **universal layer** is how you think and decide, and it upgrades anyone who reads it. The **personal layer** is you: your voice, your quirks, the specific things people learn about you the hard way. A manual with only the first reads like a leadership template. Aim for at least a third of the finished document to be unmistakably you.

## 1. How you make decisions

What you actually weigh, and in what order. Principles versus preferences, and which is which, because most teams can't tell the difference and treat both as immovable.

- What's the fastest way to convince you of something? What's the fastest way to lose you?
- What does a decision look like when you make it well? When you've made one badly, what usually went wrong?
- Where do you move fast, and where do you deliberately slow down?

*Evidence question: name the last decision you made that surprised your team, and why it surprised them.*

## 2. How you communicate

Format, length, sequence, channel. What you want first and what you want left out.

- How should someone bring you bad news? Good news? Something they're unsure about?
- What do you want in writing, and what's better said out loud?
- When you're thinking out loud rather than giving direction, how is anyone supposed to tell?

*Evidence question: what's the last message you received that you had to read three times?*

## 3. How to succeed here

What advancement actually rewards in your culture, stated plainly enough to act on. Not values. Behaviors.

- What distinguishes the people who do well around you from the people who struggle?
- What do you consistently notice and remember?
- What does someone have to demonstrate to earn more autonomy, more influence, more responsibility?

*Evidence question: who did well here recently, and what specifically did they do?*

## 4. What triggers you

Not pet peeves. Friction signals. Each one should point at something in the system that isn't working, which is what makes them fixable rather than personal.

- What reliably drains your patience, and what does it tell you about the underlying process?
- What do you find yourself doing that you shouldn't have to?

*Evidence question: what's the last thing that irritated you, and what was actually broken underneath it?*

## 5. What energizes you

The conditions that produce your best work, and the ones that quietly eat your capacity. This is the section that gets skipped, and it's the one that tells your team how to protect the thing you're actually for.

- When are you at your best? What was true about the setup that made it possible?
- What kind of work should be reaching you, and what shouldn't?

*Evidence question: what was the best working day you had in the last month, and what made it work?*

## 6. What you need from the people around you

Not deference. What real partnership looks like from where you sit.

- What does someone do that makes you trust them faster?
- What's the support that would change the most for you, that nobody has offered?
- What do you want someone to do when they disagree with you?

*Evidence question: name the last time someone made your work materially easier, and what they did.*

## 7. How you give and receive feedback

What makes it land versus bounce off, in both directions.

- How direct are you, and how should people read your directness?
- What should someone do when your feedback stings?
- How do you want to hear that you got something wrong?

*Evidence question: what's the last piece of feedback you received that changed how you work?*

## 8. Your honest, unfiltered truths

The section that makes the document yours. Quirks, contradictions, the things people misunderstand about you, and the five to seven things you learned the hard way and want to hand over.

- What do people get wrong about you at first?
- What are you actively working on in yourself?
- Where do your own stated principles contradict each other?

*Evidence question: what would your closest colleague say about you that you haven't written down here?*

**Keep the contradictions.** If two answers in this document disagree, don't resolve them into something smoother. The places where a leader's stated principles collide are the places their team spends the most energy guessing, which makes them the most valuable lines on the page.

## Before you hand it to anyone

Three checks, in this order.

**The recognition test.** Give it to one person who works closely with you and ask a single question: does this match your experience of me? Don't defend it while they answer. The gaps are the whole point, and they're the fastest edit you'll get.

**The ten-minute test.** Could someone read this in ten minutes and understand how you think and work, without a single meeting?

**The aspiration sweep.** Read it once more looking only for sentences you'd like to be true. Cut them or make them true.

Then version it and date it, the way you would any document your team is going to rely on. Michael's is on V2.2, and it changed because people told him where it was wrong.
